# Publication Pass

## Purpose

Decide whether a piece is ready to share publicly. It should sound like the
writer, communicate what they actually mean, hold up under fair scrutiny, and
be worth the attention and exposure it may bring.

This pass does not try to make everyone agree or sand off the sharp edges. The
question is whether people will disagree with the writer's real view, rather
than with something the writing accidentally suggests.

## Read the Whole Piece

Read the artifact in the form people will encounter it. For a video, consider
the words, narration, visuals, title, cover, caption, and post copy. Keep the
spoken claim separate from display-only material, and notice when packaging
makes a stronger promise than the piece itself.

## Four Tests

### Recognition

Does the writer recognize this as their own view?

Check the force of the claim, emotional posture, examples, uncertainty, and
ending. Do not make the writer sound more neutral, certain, universal, or
respectable than they are. A strong opinion can pass; a borrowed posture cannot.

### Legibility

Can a good-faith stranger understand what the writer means without filling in
the gaps with a different claim?

Name the central point, what the piece is not claiming, and any ambiguity that
would invite a predictable false reading. Add a qualification only when it
makes the intended claim more precise—not just to preempt every objection.

### Integrity

Do the claim, examples, evidence, confidence, and qualifications fit together?

Check for unsupported facts, overgeneralization, selective examples, confused
categories, and conclusions the piece has not earned. Separate disagreement
from error. A controversial claim can pass when its scope and confidence are
honest.

### Worth

Is this worth the attention, exposure, and possible disagreement required to
publish it?

Look for a live perception, concrete stake, useful distinction, or earned
personal truth. Do not demand novelty for its own sake. A familiar idea can be
worth sharing when it is expressed with unusual honesty or clarity.

## When the Piece Is Controversial

Show the disagreement clearly:

- the strongest charitable reading;
- the strongest reasonable objection;
- the most likely bad-faith misreading;
- whether each problem comes from the writing or from the reader's projection;
- the smallest clarification that would repair a writing-caused misreading.

Do not quietly soften the piece. If the remaining disagreement is a legitimate
consequence of the writer's position, say so and leave it intact.

## Judgment

Return one outcome:

- **Publish:** It sounds like the writer, stands on its own, and is worth the
  exposure.
- **Publish with qualification:** The core is ready, but one precise
  clarification is needed.
- **Revise:** The idea is worth sharing, but the current piece is not yet
  faithful, clear, or earned.
- **Do not publish:** It is not worth the exposure right now, or it captures a
  passing reaction rather than a view the writer wants to make public.

Name the decisive reason, the strongest remaining risk, and the smallest next
move. If a wording change is needed, show it and wait for the writer to adopt
it before editing authored prose.

## Review Output

Return a compact, actionable pass:

1. State the judgment first.
2. Give the public claim in one sentence.
3. Say whether it is recognizably the writer's view.
4. Give the strongest charitable reading and strongest reasonable objection.
5. Name any serious misreading and whether the writing causes it.
6. Identify the smallest needed qualification or revision, if any.
7. Say what should remain sharp or untouched because softening it would betray
   the piece.

Do not confuse “people may disagree” with “the piece is unclear.” The goal is
for criticism to land on what the writer actually chose to say.
